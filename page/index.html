The market, and why now

Everything moved in our direction in the last twelve months.

Global simulator hardware and software is a $2.1–2.4bn market, doubling by the mid-2030s; North America is ~41% of it. Korea proves people pay to hit shots indoors at scale: $1.6bn, 8,700+ venues, ~94M rounds a year — nearly twice the rounds on grass. Golfzon ~60%, Kakao VX ~25%.

America is where it happens next: 19M Americans played only off-course in 2025; simulator users went from 3.8M to 8M+ in a decade; commercial sim businesses passed 1,500 by late 2025, nearly tripled since 2022, most with 3–4 bays and three-quarters selling memberships.

Golfzon's chairman has been named Asia's most powerful golf executive three years running — and in March 2026 his company moved its Global Business Division to Virginia. Every market outside Korea is now decided in the US. That is the door.

What the tables tell us

  • The $0.75-a-round price point for an ace product is proven and familiar. That is our $1 slip.
  • Every Korean product is capped reimbursement of $112–224. Nobody sells a jackpot.
  • Golfzon already built the ace → video → insurer pipe we need. It exists for Carrot.
  • The legal wrapper that works in Korea is a short-term policy where the golfer is the policyholder — not a prize contest.
  • On Golfzon and Trackman there is no per-shot money game at all. That is the white space.

Which market first

America first. Korea second, through Golfzon HQ, as an insurance-wrapped product.

Korea isn't saturated for a jackpot; it is saturated for the reimbursement form, priced to the bone by five carriers, on a platform that already has an embedded insurer, in a legal regime where the prize-contest form hits the Criminal Act. We can win there as phase two. We should not lead with it.

The US has the untapped demand, live paid-entry precedents (Golfzon Tour, Five Iron, NEXT, Skill Strike), a mature prize-indemnity market for Santam to lead or co-insure with, every operator's decision-maker on US soil, and Ernie's strongest brand equity.

So: Golfzon stays the lead target, pitched in the US through Golfzon America. Five Iron is the fast pilot. Versant/Full Swing and Trackman run in parallel. Korea is phase two. The investor model's Korea-only volume base gets rebuilt on a US base — flagged, not done here.

Product and pricing that underwrites

The Million Dollar Shot on the slip — and the $1 door beside it.

$1,000 → $1,000,000. A 1,000× multiple prices exactly like $1 → $1,000: at Golfzon's 1 in 7,000 the insurer's expected claim is $143 a swing and a 24% cession gives a 60% loss ratio with no margin. The difference is who buys it. Nobody paying $1,000 a swing is an average golfer, so the tier binds only with one flat prize and professionals excluded (PGA members, tour status or professional prize money — declared against ID, no handicap tiers), a designated 170-yard-plus Get Lucky hole per course built into the operator's software, one attempt per round, three a day, KYC at purchase, Green-state paid entry only, and a layer above Santam's $100k single-event limit. Planning cession 45%: $250 to Get Lucky, $300 to the operator, $450 to the insurer against ~$200 of expected claims. If 0.1% of the 2029 model's entrants take one shot a year — 1,410 swings — that is $353k to us, $423k to the operator and a ~25% chance of a $1M winner, which is the content moment. Demand is unproven: launch as a flagship-venue test, verified players only.

The $1 door — the ladder everyone else plays

Golfzon's own network data, mined by GOLF.com: in 2022 users recorded 7.63 billion shots, about 1 billion of them par-3 tee shots, and 143,020 holes-in-one in completed 18-hole rounds — one ace every 6,992 par-3 swings. That is 392 aces a day, which squares with the "300+ a day" a Korean screen-golf company reported separately. A simulator ace is ~28% more likely than one outdoors (Registry: average player 12,000:1, low handicapper 5,000:1, Tour player 3,000:1).

Two cautions: 1 in 7,000 is a network average across every hole — Korea's "명당" lucky holes run far hotter, and a golfer who chooses the hole chooses the odds. And small-ticket policies like CREW ($0.86 for up to $150) are priced for expense loading, not frequency; Golfzon's data is the number.

Working assumption: base 1 in 7,000 per qualifying attempt; stress 1 in 2,500 on a player-selected hole set. Santam's measured number on our whitelist replaces it.

Legal review — United States

The case law is ours. The risk is a dozen states that never ask the skill question.

Precedent on point. Las Vegas Hacienda v. Gibson (Nev. 1961): a 50-cent entry for a $5,000 hole-in-one prize was held a feat of skill, not a gaming transaction. Cobaugh v. Klick-Lewis (Pa. 1989), Chenard v. Marcel Motors (Me. 1978) and Grove v. Charbonneau (N.D. 1976) all enforced ace prizes. Florida AGO 90-58: an entry fee that does not make up the prize is not a "stake, bet or wager" — which is exactly our insurer-funded structure, and why we are better placed in Florida than pooled-entry products.

The counter-argument is Judge Popovich's Cobaugh dissent — an ace is "such a fortuitous event that skill is almost an irrelevant factor". The answer is data: Tour players ace at 3,000:1, average golfers at 12,000:1 — a 4× skill differential — and pilot telemetry by handicap band will sharpen it.

Live comparables already map the terrain: Skill Strike runs in 44 states (out: AK, HI, MD, NV, NH, VA); Five Iron Tournaments excludes CT, DE, NV, PA, WA; Skillz excludes AR, CT, DE, LA, SD.

Two structures, one slip. A — paid $1 entry, insurer-funded prize in Green states. B — operator- or sponsor-funded free play everywhere else: the golfer pays nothing extra, consideration falls away, and only prize-promotion rules apply. Ship both in the same release so Golfzon can switch the slip on nationally and no state is a launch blocker. Full 50-state table, statutes and the counsel brief: LEGAL-RESEARCH.md in the repo (counsel to be engaged before pilot term sheets).
