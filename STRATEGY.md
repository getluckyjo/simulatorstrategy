jackpot layer inside the world's simulator platforms — a $1 swing at a real, underwritten prize,
verified by the machine, paid by the insurer — with Santam's paper behind it and Ernie Els's name on

contact with three things the August document did not have to face: the real simulator ace rate,
the competitors who already sell an insured screen ace in Korea, and the fact that nobody at

## 5. Which market first — America, and here is why

Korea is not saturated for a jackpot; it is saturated for the insurance-reimbursement form, priced
to the bone by five carriers, on a platform (Golfzon) that already has an embedded insurer, in a
legal regime where the prize-contest form hits the Criminal Act and the Speculative Acts law. We can
win there — as an insurance-wrapped product with a local carrier and Santam Re behind it — but it
is a phase-two fight and we should not lead with it.

The US is the better first bet because:

1. **The demand is there and untapped.** 19M off-course-only golfers, 8M+ simulator users,
   1,500+ commercial venues tripling in three years — and no insured ace product for consumers.
2. **The precedents are live.** Golfzon Tour, Five Iron Tournaments, NEXT and Skill Strike all run
   paid-entry real-money play with state carve-outs and facility-level responsibility. We are not
   asking a US operator to do something new; we are asking them to add a better prize.
3. **The insurance market is mature.** Prize indemnity is a regulated line (Odds On is underwritten
   by Everest, A+; Lloyd's coverholders write it). Santam can lead, co-insure or reinsure, and
   Indwe can place it.
4. **Every operator's decision-maker is in the US.** Golfzon's Global Business Division is in
   Virginia. Versant, Five Iron, Topgolf and Full Swing are American. Trackman's US sales
   leadership sits in the US.
5. **Ernie's equity is strongest there.** Two U.S. Opens, a Florida home, a Golf Channel regular —
   and Golfzon is the official simulator of the U.S. Open.
6. **One deal in the US is worth more than one deal in Korea** to the company's valuation story:
   it proves the product travels, and it is the market every other operator benchmarks.

**So: Golfzon stays the lead target, pitched in the US through Golfzon America; Five Iron is the
fast pilot; Versant/Full Swing and Trackman run in parallel. Korea is phase two, entered with
Golfzon HQ's blessing and a Korean carrier.** The current model's volume base (94M Korean rounds)
has to be rebuilt on a US base — flagged for the investor site, not done here.

## 6. Product and pricing that actually underwrites

### 6a. The ace rate

Nobody publishes a simulator ace rate, so I triangulated three signals (detail in the research file):

- A Korean screen-golf company's own data: **300+ aces a day**. Against ~94M rounds a year that is
  one ace per ~860 rounds, or **~1 in 3,400 par-3 attempts**.
- **Insurer pricing.** CREW charges ₩1,150 a round for up to ₩200,000. Even with heavy expense loads
  on a small ticket, that only makes sense at a per-round ace rate of 1-in-250 to 1-in-1,000 —
  **1 in 1,000 to 1 in 4,000 per attempt**.
- 골프단짝 pays 300× and reports golfers with "two or more aces in six months".

Against the course rate of ~1 in 12,500, simulators produce aces **4–12× more often**. And the rate
varies by an order of magnitude by hole: Korea has "명당" — short, funnel-green par-3s that produce
most of the aces — and players choose them if you let them.

**Working assumption:** base **1 in 2,500** per qualifying attempt; stress **1 in 1,000**;
optimistic 1 in 3,400. Santam's measured number replaces mine.

### 6b. What that does to $1 → $1,000 at 24%

Premium the insurer needs at a 60% target loss ratio = prize ÷ attempts-per-ace ÷ 0.6 ÷ entry:

| Ace rate | $1 → $250 | $1 → $500 | $1 → $1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 in 12,500 (course) | 3% | 7% | 13% |
| 1 in 3,434 (optimistic) | 12% | **24%** | 49% |
| 1 in 2,500 (base) | **17%** | 33% | 67% |
| 1 in 1,000 (stress) | 42% | 83% | 167% |

The August product ($1 → $1,000 at a 24% premium) prices only at course frequency. On a simulator
it fails at every plausible rate. A 500× ladder holds 24% only at the optimistic rate; **a 250×
ladder holds the 24% cession at the base rate with room to spare.**

### 6c. The pilot product

| Entry | Prize | Multiple |
|---|---|---|
| $1 | $250 | 250× |
| $2 | $500 | 250× |
| $5 | $1,250 | 250× |

with a **500× stretch ladder** ($1 → $500, $2 → $1,000, $5 → $2,500) if Santam's measured rate lands
at or below 1 in 3,400 — and the same 46 / 30 / 24 split (Get Lucky / operator / insurer). The
operator's 30% is non-negotiable in the pitch: they bring the venues, the software surface and the
customer, and we install nothing.

Rule of thumb for any conversation: **supportable prize ≈ entry × premium% × 0.6 × attempts-per-ace.**

**Product rules that are really pricing tools:**
- Qualifying-hole whitelist, set per platform from measured per-hole ace rates. No 명당.
- One insured entry per hole per round; standard difficulty, green speed and wind settings locked
  for insured shots; no mulligans, no practice-mode entries.
- Verification is the simulator's own telemetry (ball in hole, launch data, session ID, member ID)
  plus the bay camera where it exists — the "machine truth" implementation already in §24 of the
  app spec. No claim is paid on a screenshot.
- Insurer → winner, directly. Get Lucky is never in the funds flow, exactly as on course.

**Santam deliverables before any operator sees a number:**
1. Written indication at simulator frequency for a 250× and a 500× ladder, per-hole rate card.
2. Aggregate cap and largest-single-event limit for a pilot (assume 100 venues × 90 days).
3. Territory: South Africa only, or global via Santam Re / fronting / co-insurance with a US
   carrier (Everest via Odds On, or a Lloyd's coverholder) and, later, a Korean carrier.
4. Claims protocol for telemetry-based verification and a fraud clause (Korea has seen staged aces).
